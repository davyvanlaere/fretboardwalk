A minor pentatonic, with the strings running on

This is the same neck with the guitar's irregularity put back in: the string above the G is a B, a major third up, where every other step is a fourth. Everything from there up is tuned a semitone low, so the same note is found one fret further along — and the tiles that straddle that step come out bent. Same scale, same two tiles, same count of them; only the neck under them has a kink.

A guitar has six strings and one irregularity: the jump from G to B is a major third where every other jump is a fourth. This neck has no such limit — as many strings as you ask for, each a fourth above the last. The guitar sits in the middle of them, its six strings named in full down the left-hand edge, starting E A D G and then carrying on in fourths where the real instrument would break. The dim strings above and below are the ones it hasn't got: the same run, continuing past both ends.

What the colours are

Every note of A minor pentatonic — A, C, D, E and G — is lit, with the root A in cyan. The two notes it leaves out of its parent scale, B and F, are drawn half transparent: put them back and the five notes become A natural minor, the same seven as C major.

Each one is labelled with its degree counted twice, written major|minor. The first number is where the note falls in the parent major key, C; the second is where the same note falls in that key's relative minor, A — one set of notes, numbered from two different roots. So the root of this page's scale reads 6|1: the sixth of C major and the first of A minor. The pentatonic is then 6|1 1|3 2|4 3|5 5|7, and the two half-transparent notes are the degrees it skips, 7|2 and 4|6. The overlays are drawn from the five lit notes alone: a half-transparent note often falls inside a box, but it is never one of its corners — the pattern below is a property of the five-note scale, and stops being one the moment these two join in.

Once the whole neck is drawn, the scale stops looking like five separate box shapes to memorise and starts looking like one pattern made of two tiles, laid end to end for ever:

  • The three-string tile — three neighbouring strings that all carry the same two frets, two frets apart. Three strings wide, a whole-step tall.
  • The two-string tile — two neighbouring strings that both carry the same two frets, three frets apart. Two strings wide, a minor-third tall.

The tiles have no outlines: where two of them overlap, their colours add. Where the two kinds share a column of frets, that column lights up in a colour of its own, and two tiles of the same kind sharing one simply brighten it. So every edge on the board is a change of colour, and the colour tells you what is stacked there.

Between them they account for every note of the pentatonic — there is no lit note that isn't a corner of at least one tile. Walk up any single string and each step you take is the edge of exactly one tile, so the colours you cross simply spell out the scale's own intervals: minor third, tone, tone, minor third, tone, over and over. Where two tiles meet they share a fret, and the note on it belongs to both.

On a real guitar this stops being true halfway up. The B string is a major third above the G, not a fourth, so every tile from there up slides one fret towards the bridge — and one pattern becomes five positions, each needing its own small correction on the top two strings.
