How Fretboardwalk works — and why the major scale matters

Fretboardwalk is a free trainer that teaches you where every note and scale degree lives on the guitar neck — in any key. This guide explains how to play and why the major scale is the single most useful thing a beginning guitarist can learn.

Hear it

Real guitar, same sound as the trainer. The major pentatonic is just the major scale with the 4th and 7th taken out — five notes instead of seven.

What is Fretboardwalk?

Most guitarists learn a handful of chord shapes and scale patterns but never really know the fretboard — which note is under any given finger, or how it relates to the key they are playing in. Fretboardwalk turns that knowledge into a quick, game-like drill. You are shown a target and you find it on the neck; the app plays the real note back so your ears and your hands learn together.

How to play

  1. Pick a key at the top (start with C major). Every target is measured relative to that key.
  2. Read the two panels. Current shows the scale degree you are standing on. Find shows the degree you need to locate next.
  3. Tap that degree on the neck. A scale degree appears in several places across the strings — any correct spot counts.
  4. Hear it. A correct tap plays the real guitar note at that fret, so you connect the number, the sound, and the position all at once.
  5. Keep the streak going. Each correct answer moves you forward and gives you a new target. Build the longest streak you can.

Make it harder as you improve

  • Note display: switch from Numerals (the answer is written on the board) to Dots (positions shown, but not which degree) to Hidden (nothing shown — pure recall).
  • Lowered degrees: add ♭3 ♭6 ♭7 once the seven major-scale degrees feel automatic.
  • Time Attack: a one-life mode where every correct note shortens the clock. See how far you get before it catches you — each display mode keeps its own top-5 scores.

What are scale degrees?

A scale degree is just a number that names a note by its place in the scale instead of by its letter. The major scale has seven notes, so we number them 1 through 7. In C major that looks like this:

*The 4 is the subdominant and the 5 the dominant — the two degrees that, with the 1, form the classic three-chord songwriting core.

The magic is that these numbers don't change when you change key. The 1 is always "home" (the root, or tonic). The 5 always creates tension that wants to resolve back to 1. Learn to think in degrees and you learn music once, not twelve times over.

Why the major scale matters for beginning guitarists

The major scale is the ruler that the rest of Western music is measured against. Almost everything you will run into as a guitarist is described in relation to it:

  • Chords come from it. Stack degrees and you get chords: 1–3–5 is the major chord that gives the key its name; 2–4–6 is a minor chord, and so on. The famous I–IV–V and ii–V–I progressions are just scale degrees turned into chords.
  • Keys and transposing. "Play it in G instead of C" only makes sense if you think in degrees. Move the numbers, keep the relationships, and the song works in any key.
  • Soloing and melody. When you know that the note you are on is the 5 or the 3, you can aim for the notes that sound good over a chord instead of hoping a memorized shape lands somewhere sensible.
  • It transfers everywhere. Minor keys, the modes, pentatonic and blues scales, and even jazz harmony are all described as alterations of the major scale (that's what the ♭3 / ♭6 / ♭7 option hints at).

The bottleneck for most beginners isn't knowing that the major scale matters — it's connecting the abstract number to an actual fret, fast, without counting up from the open string every time. That instant link between degree → sound → position is exactly what Fretboardwalk drills.

The major scale is the parent of every other scale

This is the single idea worth burning into memory: almost every scale you will ever learn is just the major scale with some notes changed, removed, or started from a different point. Learn the major scale well and you are not learning one scale — you are learning the map that all the others are drawn on.

  • Major pentatonic is the major scale with the 4 and 7 removed (1 2 3 5 6) — five "safe" notes. Use the Hear it player at the top to play them back to back.
  • The natural minor scale is the major scale with a lowered 3rd, 6th and 7th (♭3 ♭6 ♭7).
  • The modes — Dorian, Mixolydian and the rest — are the exact same major-scale notes, just treating a different degree as home.
  • Minor pentatonic and the blues scale are small, bluesy edits of that same framework.

So the fastest way to all of those isn't memorizing each one from scratch — it is knowing the major scale so well that every other scale becomes a small adjustment to a shape you already own.

How to practice: the three-stage ladder

The Scale notes on the neck setting (in the trainer's settings menu) has three modes — and they are meant to be climbed in order, from most help to none. Start on the first rung and only move up once the current one feels easy. Jumping straight to Hidden as a beginner is like trying to run before you can walk.

  1. Numerals — start here. Every scale note shows its degree number right on the board, so the answer is always in front of you. This stage builds the map with zero pressure: you learn where each degree lives just by playing. Stay here until you can find the target without hunting for it.
  2. Dots only — the bridge. The scale positions are still marked, but the numbers disappear. Now you know where the notes are but have to recall which degree each one is. This is the step between reading and remembering.
  3. Hidden — the goal. Nothing is shown at all. You find every degree from memory. When you can play comfortably in Hidden mode, you genuinely know the fretboard.

Two patterns are worth learning early, because between them they are what makes the top two rungs realistic — one for each direction you can move on the neck:

  • Along a string — the major scale formula. The gaps between consecutive degrees are W W H W W W H: a whole step is two frets, a half step is one. Only 3→4 and 7→1 are half steps; everything else is two frets.
  • Across the strings — the sequence 7 3 6 2 5 1 4. At the same fret, each string over is the next number along, so you can find a degree's neighbours without counting up from the root.

Reach across at the same fret to get close, then slide a fret or two to land — that is the whole method, and it's what the trainer's How do I find it? hint walks you through. The major/minor degree map covers both patterns in full, including the two places the sequence needs an exception.

Two habits that speed the whole climb up:

  • Change keys once a mode feels easy. Switching keys proves you learned the pattern, not a fixed set of dots in one spot.
  • Keep sessions short. A few focused minutes a day beats one long grind. Use Time Attack to check whether your recall is actually getting faster.

What it's all for

Recall for its own sake gets dull fast, so it helps to see what the skill buys you. The clearest early payoff is chords: label a barre chord with degrees and it stops being a picture you memorised. The 3 is on one particular string, and moving that one finger gives you minor, sus2 or sus4 — no new shapes. Drop the spare octave and you have a 7th. Building chords from degrees walks through both barre shapes, one finger at a time.

FAQ

Do I need to read sheet music?

No. Everything happens on the fretboard and by ear — you tap frets and hear the real note. No notation required.

What exactly is a scale degree?

A number (1–7) that names a note by its position in the scale rather than its letter. In C major, C is the 1, D is the 2, and so on — and those numbers stay the same in every key. For a full beginner walkthrough, see scale degrees explained.

Why learn degrees instead of note names?

Note names change with every key; degrees don't. Thinking in degrees lets you transpose, understand chord progressions, build chords, and target solo notes — skills that work across all 12 keys at once. For a concrete example, see how one barre shape becomes twelve chords once you know which finger is on which degree.

Shouldn't I learn the pentatonic scale first?

Many players do, and that's fine — it's only five notes and gets you soloing fast. But the pentatonic isn't a rival to the major scale; it's a piece of it (the major pentatonic is the major scale minus the 4th and 7th). More on how they fit together in the pentatonic scale guide.

What about the minor scale?

The natural minor scale is just the major scale with three lowered degrees (♭3 ♭6 ♭7) — the ♭3 is what makes it sound sad. See the minor scale guide for how it relates to major.

Which key should I start in?

C major — no sharps or flats, so the relationships are easiest to see. Then switch keys to test that you really understand the pattern.

Is Fretboardwalk free?

Yes. It runs in your browser and is free to use.
